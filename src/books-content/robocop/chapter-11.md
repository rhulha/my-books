Chapter 11 - Going Home.

Dr. Ellison had a list.

He went through it standing at the foot of Alex's bed, not looking up.

No extreme temperatures. The joint seals were rated to minus twenty but heat above sixty degrees Celsius could compromise the fluid system. Avoid prolonged immersion in water — the chassis was splash-proof, not waterproof. No lifting more than four hundred kilos until Kam cleared him for higher loads. Call immediately if the vision feed flickered or he heard a grinding from the hip joints.

He turned a page.

"Power." He tapped the item without looking up. "The chassis runs on OCP CoreCell. A solid-state graphene cell array. Full charge takes four hours. Under normal conditions it will last forty to forty-eight hours, but I recommend you recharge every night."

Ellen wrote something in the folder.

"In exceptional circumstances — extended field operations, a lost night — you have a fifty hour ceiling before the system begins pulling reserve power from non-essential functions. Navigation, thermal imaging, some motor precision. That's your one exception. One."

He moved on without pausing.

"The perfusate." He gestured toward a hard-sided case on the chair by the window. Grey, about the size of a carry-on, with an OCP serial number on the latch. "Your brain is kept in a closed-circuit neural perfusate, pumped continuously — a temperature-regulated nutrient and oxygenation solution. It needs to be exchanged every twenty-four hours. There are thirty units in that case. A resupply will be delivered to your home on the same schedule."

He looked up for the first time.

"This one is not negotiable, and I want you both to understand why. The perfusate degrades. It's a biological medium — it accumulates metabolic waste, loses buffering capacity, pH drifts. At twenty-four hours the degradation is manageable. At thirty-six hours you will notice cognitive effects. At forty-eight hours—" He stopped. "Brain tissue doesn't warn you before it starts dying. It just does. The degradation past forty-eight hours is exponential, not linear. There is no recovery from that curve."

Ellen had stopped writing.

"Change it every day. Set a reminder. Treat it like oxygen." He looked back at the list. "Questions?"

"No," said Alex.

"Good luck, Alex."

They shook hands — or tried to. Dr. Ellison wrapped his fingers around two of Alex's and gave a quick squeeze.

Kam held the door. A nurse handed Ellen a folder of documents.

The corridor was quiet until they reached the lobby.

Through the glass doors Alex could see them waiting. Cameras, microphones, a wall of people.

"Ready?" said Kam.

"No," said Alex.

Kam pushed the door open anyway.

The noise hit first. Then the light.

"Mr. Murphy! How does it feel to leave the hospital?"

"Are you going back to the force?"

"How does it feel to be a cyborg?"

"Are you still human, Mr. Murphy?"

Alex kept walking. The crowd pressed in from both sides and Kam cut a path through with his shoulder.

"No comment," said Kam, not breaking stride.

The van was parked at the curb. White, large, with OCP's logo on the side panel.

The side door opened and a wide chair on a mechanical arm descended to street level.

Alex turned, backed up to the chair and sat down.

The arm lifted him smoothly and folded him inside.

Ellen climbed into the driver's seat without a word. Kam closed the side door from the outside.

He tapped the window.

Ellen lowered it.

"You've got my number," said Kam. "Both of you."

"Thank you," said Ellen. "For everything."

Kam looked past her at Alex. "See you Thursday."

"See you Thursday," said Alex.

Kam stepped back and Ellen pulled out.

News vans were parked the full length of Primrose Lane. Alex counted seven from the window.

Ellen slowed to a crawl. A man with a camera stepped off the curb and she had to brake hard.

"Unbelievable," she said under her breath.

She found a gap and pulled into the driveway.

The front door was already open. Jamie was standing in the doorway in a hoodie and jeans.

She looked at the van.

Ellen got out first. The ramp came down and the chair lowered Alex to the driveway.

He stood up.

From behind him a voice shouted, "Mr. Murphy! How does it feel to be home?"

He didn't answer. He walked to the front door.

Jamie's eyes were wide. She was trying to keep her face neutral and not quite managing it.

"Hey," said Alex.

"Hey Dad." Her voice was almost steady.

He stopped in front of her.

She looked at his chest, his arms, his hands. Then back at his face.

She started to reach for him, then stopped. Her hands dropped to her sides.

She looked at the ground.

Alex waited.

Then Jamie stepped forward and wrapped her arms as far around him as they would go, pressing her face against the cold metal of his chest.

Alex lifted one arm and held her.

"Ow," she said, her voice muffled.

"Sorry." He loosened his grip.

She didn't let go.
