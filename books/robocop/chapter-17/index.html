Back on the Beat

Chapter 17 - Back on the Beat.

Anne Lewis had been a Detroit beat cop for eleven years, which meant she had seen, more or less, every species of weirdness the city could put together on a budget. Domestic disturbances at 3 a.m. that ended with both spouses asking her out for breakfast. A man trying to barter a live raccoon for a six-pack at a Mobil station. A funeral procession that turned into a fistfight that turned into a second, smaller funeral procession.

She had not, however, ever had a partner who weighed three hundred and seventy pounds and was made of metal.

The Ford Taurus was new. Black, big, with the dashboard set deeper than usual and a suspension that had been reinforced for extreme heavy duty.

The passenger seats had been removed entirely and replaced with something that looked like a cross between a barber's chair and the cockpit of a fighter jet. But the car was also equipped with an insert that turned one of these cockpits into a reasonably standard passenger seat.

"How's it riding?" she asked, easing through a yellow on Woodward.

"Decent," said Alex.

"Yeah?"

"Yeah. I mean I'm happy I can drive at all. Not the time to be picky."

Anne nodded.

He had a way of putting things, she'd noticed. He still sounded like Alex. He still made the same kind of jokes.
But there was something else in the way he said things, something that made it sound like he was saying them for the first time, or like he was saying them to himself as much as to her. It was a little disconcerting, but it also made her feel like she was getting to know him again, in a way.

The diner was at Woodward and Mack. It was called Sully's, though no one named Sully had owned it since around the time the Reagan administration had ended.

He took the entrance ramp around the side of the building.
There was a drive-through speaker on a pole with a cracked plastic menu above it.

He pulled up and stopped. The Taurus sat high enough that the speaker came to about his shoulder.

"Welcome to Sully's, what can I get you?" said a voice through the static.

"Coffee," Alex said. "Large. Cream and sugar."

A pause.

"That's it?"

"That's it."

He pulled forward. Anne dug cash out of her jacket. The window slid open and a teenager in a grease-spotted apron handed over a cardboard cup. Then he looked up and his mouth opened wide.

"RoboCop," he said.

Alex took the cup.

"Have a good one," Alex said, pushing down on the accelerator.

He handed the cup to Anne and pulled back out onto Woodward.

Then he heard it.

Not words. Something between a scuffle and a struggle, low and close, coming from the narrow gap between the dumpsters and the back wall of the diner. The kind of sound that your brain tries to file under something else and can't quite manage.

He stopped the cruiser.

"Murphy—"

"Yeah."

He was already out.

The gap was maybe four feet wide. Two men had a woman pressed against the brickwork. She wasn't making much noise anymore. One of the men heard Alex coming and turned around and started to say something, and Alex grabbed him by the collar and the belt and threw him. Not hard. Not relative to what Alex could do. The man sailed about fifteen feet and landed in a rolling heap against a chain-link fence and lay there making a sound like a deflating tire.

The second one didn't wait. He was already running before the first one hit the ground. He made it to the end of the alley and kept going, and Alex let him go, because the woman was sliding down the wall and he needed to not be throwing people anymore.

Anne was already there. She'd come around the other side at a run, badge out, and now she was crouching in front of the woman with her hand out.

"Hey. Hey, look at me. You're okay. We've got you."

The woman let Anne take her arm. She was young, maybe 19. Her dress was torn at the shoulder. She got her feet under her slowly.

"Are you all right?" Anne asked.

She sniffed and nodded. She didn't say anything.

"Would you like to go to a hospital?"

She quickly shook her head.

"Would you like to go to the station and make a report?"

She shook her head again. Everyone here knew nothing would come of it.

"Can we drive you somewhere?"

She shook her head again.

Then she opened her mouth after all. "Can I have a few bucks for the diner?"

"Sure," said Anne and pulled out a five-dollar bill.

The young woman wanted to stuff it into her pocket, but just as she straightened up, something fell out of her pocket and hit the asphalt with a small, bright click.

A red vial.

If Alex still had a heart, it would've sunk.

"What's your name?" Alex asked, crouching down to pick it up.

She looked at the vial, cursing her luck, knowing she would not get it back, and then she looked at Alex.

"Sarah," she said.

Alex didn't know if it was the truth or not. It didn't matter.

He held up the vial.

"Sarah, this shit is liquid death. It's poison, it's a money pit."

The woman looked at him. She had a bruise coming up along her jaw.

"I know," she said defeated.

He stayed crouched for a moment. He didn't say anything.

She glanced at Anne, then back at Alex.

"Sarah." He looked at the vial. "I'm not going to arrest you. I'm going to ask you to do something instead. I'm going to ask you to please — please — stop putting this into your body. Whatever's going on. Whatever reason you started. Nothing good is ever going to come out of this. Nothing. You understand me?"

She didn't answer. She was looking at the vial in his hand.

Clearly longing for him to give it back to her.

He snapped his fingers, turning the vial into a thin mist.

"Sarah."

She looked at him, bitter and angry.

She gathered her bag from the ground. She straightened her dress at the shoulder. And without looking at either one again, she walked to the end of the alley, turned the corner and was gone.

Alex looked at Anne with bitterness in his mind.

"We gotta do something about this shit!"
